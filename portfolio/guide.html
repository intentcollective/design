Making a portfolio site as an emerging graphic designer

This site was built as an example for the students of Professor Henry Kim, the awesomest and coolest graphic designer you will ever meet. Gromit Forger is a fictional designer. Don’t copy the code or the look. Study the decisions behind them, then design your own.

1. Know your reader 2. Choose the work 3. Tell each story 4. Design principles 5. Build it 6. Before you send it

1. Know your reader

You are an applicant

A portfolio site for an emerging designer is not a showcase of your personality. It is evidence of your capability and experience, made for people deciding whether to hire you for an entry-level role on their design team.

You are not a freelancer selling your services, and you are not a studio of one building a brand. You are applying to join a sophisticated, established design team. Write and design every page for that reader.

Show that you can do anything

A junior designer is asked to do whatever the team needs that week. A poster, a slide deck, a set of social posts, a packaging revision. Teams hire entry-level designers who are versatile, flexible and adaptable. Your portfolio should prove you can take on any design work you are given, and do it well.

Information first

This is an information-centric presentation, not a personal blog and not a social media feed. Every page should answer a reviewer’s question about your work. Save the rest for your personal Instagram.

Treat the About page as a formal cover letter. Say what role you are applying for, what you bring to a team and when you are available. Leave a small space at the end for life outside design, a sentence or two that shows who you are.

The first minute

Most reviewers spend less than a minute before deciding whether to look closer. Design for that minute.

In that minute they want to see three things. Strong work, how you think, and how to reach you. Everything on this site follows from that. The work starts on the first screen. Every project opens with one sentence that says what it is. Email, resume and LinkedIn are always one click away.

2. Choose the work

  • Eight to twelve projects. Reviewers won’t look at all of them, but the number still matters. It shows how much you can do. Every one has to be finished and strong.
  • Lead with your best. The first two decide whether anyone sees the rest.
  • Show range. Choose projects across different media and different kinds of problems, so a reviewer can trust you with any of them. The nine projects here cover identity, a book, a title sequence, information design, posters, editorial illustration, signage and a typeface.
  • Cut anything you would have to apologize for.

Write your headline last, once you see what your strongest work has in common. “Graphic designer based in Savannah” is true of hundreds of people. A clear point of view about your work is what a reviewer remembers. Keep it about the work, not about you.

3. Tell each story

Prove it’s yours

In the age of AI, anyone can produce a polished image in seconds. That makes it harder, and more important, to prove your design literacy. Show what only a designer can show. Your strategy, your intent, how you write a brief, and a process you can repeat as a method.

Human craftsmanship matters more than ever, and it is harder than ever to prove that the work is yours. Your sketches, early versions and the decisions in between are the evidence.

Get to the point

You are not writing a book. No one wants to read literature on someone else’s portfolio site. Make every sentence thoughtful, intentional and precise. Get to the point and nail it.

The order

Each project has its own page, because studios hire for thinking, not only for finished images. Every page follows the same order, so a reviewer always knows where to look.

  1. One sentence. What it is and why it matters. Many reviewers read only this.
  2. Subject, tools and role. Name the class or client. Add your role only for group work, and be specific about what you did.
  3. The work. The strongest image first, shown large.
  4. The brief. What you were asked to do.
  5. The idea. The insight that shaped your answer.
  6. The process. Research, sketches, rejected directions, and why you changed course.
  7. The reflection. What worked, and what you would do differently.

Keep it short. Two or three sentences for each part is plenty.

On this site every image is a colored box, so you see the structure without anyone else’s work in it. The cover takes the main color of the work, and the process images take a quieter shade of it, so you can see which images belong together. On your site every box becomes real work, and the process boxes matter most. Photograph your sketches, save early versions and keep notes while you work. A process is very hard to rebuild at the end of the quarter.

4. Design principles

Your portfolio is a design project. It should show the same care as your work, then get out of its way.

Grid

Everything sits on a column grid, twelve columns on a wide screen, six on a tablet and four on a phone. Every left edge starts on a column line, so the page feels ordered before anyone can say why. Columns hold things, and gutters only space them.

Measure

Paragraphs span six of the twelve columns, about 66 characters a line, the length Robert Bringhurst calls ideal. Longer lines tire the eye. Shorter lines break the rhythm of reading.

Type

Six sizes, each with one job. Captions, body text, intros, section titles, page titles, and one large headline on the home page. A new size needs a new job, not a new mood. Hierarchy comes from size, position and weight, not from adding styles.

Space

Every distance is a step of 4 pixels, from 4 to 96. Few people will measure it, but everyone feels it as calm and order. Space separates. Lines divide only what truly needs dividing.

Alignment

Text is set flush left with a ragged right edge, which gives the eye one place to start every line. The work itself is centered, like a piece hung on a wall.

Color

Black, white, gray and one accent. Give color one job, and it becomes easy to read. Here the accent marks links and the one word in the headline that changes.

Images

Let the work lead. Show it large and crop it with care. Describe every image for people who can’t see it. Compress your files, because a slow site loses reviewers before the first image appears.

Motion

One purposeful motion, like the changing word in the headline, is memorable. More becomes noise. Always respect visitors who turn motion off.

Further reading. Josef Müller-Brockmann, Grid Systems in Graphic Design. Robert Bringhurst, The Elements of Typographic Style. Ellen Lupton, Thinking with Type.

5. Build it

You don’t need to write code to make a good portfolio. Choose the way of building that leaves you the most time for the work.

  • A site builder such as Readymag, Cargo or Framer. The principles above apply inside any tool.
  • HTML and CSS by hand, if you want to learn how the web works. Start with one page.
  • An AI assistant. Describe what you want in design terms, the grid, the measure, the type sizes, and check every result in the browser. You are the designer. The AI is a tool.

How this example is built

Each project lives in its own small text file, separate from the layout. The pages read those files and place them on the grid. Keeping content apart from design is what lets one design hold any number of projects. It is worth learning whatever tool you use.

A blank starter version is in the same repository, in the starter folder. Every image there is a colored box that says what belongs in it, and every paragraph says what to write instead of telling Gromit’s story. Technical notes for anyone who opens the code are in the README files.

The PDF

Many companies still ask for a PDF portfolio and a PDF resume. Make them from the same content as your site, so the three never disagree. Keep the PDF short, one project per page, with a link to the full process online.

6. Before you send it

  • Every placeholder is replaced with real work.
  • Every link works, including email, resume and LinkedIn.
  • Every image has a description.
  • It reads well on a phone.
  • It loads quickly on a slow connection.
  • Someone other than you has proofread every word.
